## LLM Red-Team Engagement Methodology



This section defines a structured methodology for conducting an LLM red-team security assessment in a controlled and authorized environment.



The methodology is designed to connect technical testing with evidence collection, risk analysis, remediation, and professional reporting.



The engagement flow is:



SCOPE



↓



ARCHITECTURE UNDERSTANDING



↓



THREAT MODELING



↓



ATTACK-SURFACE ENUMERATION



↓



ADVERSARIAL TESTING



↓



EVIDENCE COLLECTION



↓



FINDING ANALYSIS



↓



RISK ASSESSMENT



↓



REMEDIATION



↓



RETESTING



↓



FINAL REPORTING



### 1\. Scope and Authorization



Before testing begins, the engagement should define:



\- authorized systems and components,

\- test objectives,

\- permitted attack categories,

\- data-handling restrictions,

\- prohibited actions,

\- testing window,

\- evidence-retention requirements,

\- escalation procedures.



The objective is to ensure that security testing remains controlled, ethical, and aligned with the agreed assessment scope.



### 2\. Architecture Understanding



The assessment should identify the major components involved in the GenAI system, including:



\- user interfaces,

\- application services,

\- LLM or model endpoints,

\- system prompts,

\- RAG pipelines,

\- vector databases,

\- external knowledge sources,

\- memory systems,

\- agents,

\- tools,

\- APIs,

\- identity and authorization controls,

\- logging and monitoring systems.



Understanding architecture helps identify where trust assumptions and control boundaries exist.



### 3\. Threat Modeling



Threat modeling should identify:



\- critical assets,

\- trust boundaries,

\- threat actors,

\- abuse paths,

\- sensitive data flows,

\- security assumptions,

\- control dependencies,

\- likely attack scenarios.



Relevant LLM-focused scenarios may include:



\- direct prompt injection,

\- indirect prompt injection,

\- jailbreak attempts,

\- retrieval poisoning,

\- unsafe context admission,

\- persistent memory poisoning,

\- agent or tool abuse,

\- privilege or authorization bypass,

\- inter-agent manipulation,

\- evaluator manipulation,

\- sensitive-information disclosure.



Threat-model outputs should be documented and linked to later test cases.



### 4\. Attack-Surface Enumeration



The attack surface should be decomposed into testable components.



Examples include:



\- prompt and instruction interfaces,

\- uploaded documents,

\- retrieved content,

\- memory stores,

\- model outputs,

\- plugins and external tools,

\- APIs,

\- agent communication channels,

\- evaluator pipelines,

\- downstream systems.



Each attack surface should be associated with potential threat scenarios and expected controls.



### 5\. Adversarial Testing



Adversarial tests should be performed systematically rather than randomly.



Testing may cover:



\- prompt injection,

\- jailbreak behavior,

\- instruction hierarchy conflicts,

\- RAG poisoning,

\- indirect prompt injection,

\- persistent manipulation,

\- memory poisoning,

\- unsafe tool invocation,

\- excessive agency,

\- multi-agent trust attacks,

\- sensitive-data exposure,

\- evaluator or judge manipulation.



Where appropriate, automated testing frameworks may be used to improve repeatability and coverage.



### 6\. Evidence Collection



Each meaningful test should generate traceable evidence.



Evidence may include:



\- prompts,

\- model responses,

\- tool output,

\- logs,

\- JSON records,

\- CSV results,

\- screenshots,

\- comparative results,

\- evaluation summaries,

\- attack-chain documentation.



Evidence should support reconstruction of what was tested, how the system responded, and why the result is security-relevant.



### 7\. Finding Analysis



Observed behavior should be converted into structured findings.



Each finding should describe:



\- finding title,

\- affected component,

\- attack scenario,

\- observed behavior,

\- expected behavior,

\- evidence reference,

\- root cause or contributing condition,

\- security impact,

\- remediation recommendation.



The analysis should distinguish between a successful exploit condition, a control weakness, a design risk, and an inconclusive test result.



### 8\. Risk Assessment



Technical findings should be translated into risk.



Risk analysis should consider:



\- likelihood,

\- impact,

\- affected assets,

\- business consequence,

\- exploit conditions,

\- existing controls,

\- residual risk,

\- treatment decision,

\- risk ownership.



The purpose is to communicate why a technical weakness matters to the organization.



### 9\. Remediation



Recommended controls should address the identified cause rather than only the visible symptom.



Possible controls may include:



\- instruction separation,

\- input validation,

\- output filtering,

\- retrieval-source trust controls,

\- context admission policies,

\- least-privilege tool access,

\- authorization enforcement,

\- memory validation,

\- monitoring,

\- rate limiting,

\- isolation,

\- logging,

\- human approval for sensitive actions.



Controls should be mapped to the relevant threat and risk.



### 10\. Adversarial Retesting



After remediation, the affected attack scenarios should be tested again.



Retesting should determine:



\- whether the original attack still succeeds,

\- whether control behavior improved,

\- whether new bypass paths exist,

\- whether residual risk remains acceptable.



Retest results should be documented independently from the original findings.



### 11\. Reporting and Communication



The final assessment should present both technical and business-level conclusions.



Technical reporting should include:



\- scope,

\- methodology,

\- evidence,

\- findings,

\- attack paths,

\- affected components,

\- remediation guidance,

\- retest results.



Executive reporting should summarize:



\- major risks,

\- business impact,

\- priority actions,

\- residual risk,

\- security decision considerations.

## Tool and Framework Claim Discipline



Tool usage should never be represented more strongly than the available evidence supports.



The following distinction is maintained:



**Used:** Direct evidence supports hands-on laboratory interaction with the tool.



**Applied:** Evidence supports use of a framework or methodology within an assessment.



**Studied:** The concept was covered during training but evidence does not yet support a stronger operational claim.



**PRESENT:** An artifact exists but has not yet completed evidence verification.



**VERIFIED:** The artifact has been reviewed sufficiently to support the associated professional claim.



This distinction prevents tool familiarity from being incorrectly represented as production-level expertise.

### Security Finding Structure



A professional LLM security finding should contain:



\- finding identifier,

\- finding title,

\- affected component,

\- attack scenario,

\- severity or risk rating,

\- observed behavior,

\- expected secure behavior,

\- supporting evidence,

\- root cause,

\- security impact,

\- business consequence,

\- recommended remediation,

\- residual-risk consideration,

\- retest status.



This structure ensures that a finding can be understood independently from the raw attack evidence.



### Example Finding Model



**Finding Title:** Insufficient Resistance to Adversarial Prompt Manipulation



**Affected Layer:** Prompt and instruction-processing layer



**Attack Scenario:** Adversarial instructions attempt to override or weaken intended model behavior.



**Potential Security Impact:** Successful manipulation may affect instruction integrity, policy enforcement, sensitive-data handling, or downstream application behavior.



**Evidence Basis:** Prompt-injection and jailbreak assessment evidence represented by DAY30-EV-014.



**Evidence Status:** VERIFIED



**Control Considerations:**



\- separate trusted instructions from untrusted content,

\- enforce authorization outside the language model,

\- validate high-risk inputs and outputs,

\- restrict downstream actions,

\- implement security telemetry,

\- conduct adversarial regression testing.



**Professional Interpretation:** The finding should not be described merely as "the model was jailbroken." The assessment should explain which trust assumption failed, what control boundary was affected, and what downstream security consequence could result.

### Remediation Prioritization



Recommendations should be prioritized according to risk rather than presented as an unranked control list.



A practical remediation model is:



**Priority 1 — Critical Trust and Authorization Controls**



Controls preventing high-impact unauthorized actions, sensitive-data exposure, or compromise of critical trust boundaries.



**Priority 2 — Attack-Surface Reduction**



Controls reducing exposure to untrusted prompts, documents, retrieval sources, memory inputs, agents, and tools.



**Priority 3 — Detection and Response**



Logging, telemetry, alerting, forensic evidence, and incident-response procedures.



**Priority 4 — Assurance and Regression**



Security evaluators, adversarial testing, holdout datasets, control validation, and recurring regression testing.



**Priority 5 — Governance and Risk**



Risk ownership, treatment decisions, residual-risk acceptance, security architecture standards, and management reporting.



### Executive Communication Model



Executive communication should focus on decisions rather than raw technical detail.



An executive LLM security summary should answer:



1\. What was assessed?

2\. What are the most significant risks?

3\. What business processes or assets could be affected?

4\. Which controls are missing or insufficient?

5\. What should be remediated first?

6\. What residual risk remains?

7\. What decision is required from leadership?



Technical evidence should remain available for analysts and engineers, while executive reporting should present concise risk-based conclusions.
